Would You Let an LLM Into a Safety-Critical Calculation?

2026-08-08 · 4 min read AI engineering energy safety-critical verification

Here's a question I keep coming back to as a power engineer who also builds AI tools: would you let a language model into a calculation where a wrong answer is a safety incident?

Most people answer one of two ways. "No, never" — and they miss where AI genuinely helps. Or "sure, the models are good now" — and they're trusting a probabilistic text generator with a number that has to be right, not plausible. Both answers miss the actual move.

The failure mode isn't "wrong." It's "confidently wrong."¶

A safety-critical engineering calculation has a property most software doesn't: you often can't tell a good answer from a bad one by reading it. A clearance, a rating, a fault level — the wrong number looks exactly as authoritative as the right one. That's the whole danger. An LLM is very good at producing authoritative-looking output, which means a more eloquent model can make the problem worse, not better.

So the instinct everyone reaches for — "use a stronger model, add more reasoning" — doesn't address the real risk. A better model lowers the error rate; it doesn't tell you which outputs are the errors. For a regulated calculation, an unknown error rate is the same as no answer at all.

The pattern: a validity gate the model can't talk its way past¶

The discipline that makes AI usable in safety-critical work is the opposite of trusting it more: refuse to trust any output until it passes a deterministic check you control.

The model proposes; physics and the standard dispose:

  1. The model generates a candidate — an implementation, a number, a derivation.
  2. A deterministic gate validates it — not another LLM, not a judgment call. Real checks: unit/dimensional consistency, physical bounds (no negative resistances, no super-luminal anything), conservation laws, and — the strongest one — agreement with published reference cases from the governing standard, to a tolerance.
  3. Hard fail → regenerate or escalate to a human. Soft smell → surface a warning, don't silently pass.
  4. Only a candidate that clears the gate is ever shown as an answer.

The model is the generator. The gate is ground truth. The eloquence of the model is irrelevant to whether the answer is trusted, because the answer earns trust by passing a check, not by sounding right.

Why this also changes the "which model" question¶

Once a validity gate exists, the model becomes the cheap, swappable part. This is the through-line from a benchmark I ran recently on whether the model or the harness is the bottleneck: when the loop around the model verifies the output, a much cheaper model often does the job, because the gate catches errors regardless of which model produced them. Verification architecture is what lets you stop overpaying for raw model strength.

This isn't an AI idea. It's how engineering already works.¶

The reason this pattern feels obvious to an engineer is that it's the same instinct we apply without AI: you don't trust a calculation, you verify it — against the standard, against a hand check, against a reference case. The validity gate is just that instinct, automated, sitting between a fast generator and a human decision.

Which is also why I think "AI for the grid" (or for any safety-critical domain) is mostly not a model problem — it's a verification-architecture problem. The interesting, durable work sits elsewhere: building the deterministic gates, grounding the generation in the actual standards, and designing the regenerate-and-escalate loop so a confident-but-wrong answer never reaches the field. That's the work I find worth doing — bringing software/AI rigor to engineering, with the engineer still owning the judgment.

The model can draft. It just doesn't get to decide.
